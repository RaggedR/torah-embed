Contrastive Retrieval over the Babylonian Talmud}

code development, experiment execution and manuscript preparation.}}
\date{24 September 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We observe that the \emph{ein mishpat / ner mitsvah} apparatus --- a
cross-reference system printed in the margins of the Vilna Talmud, mapping
passages of Talmudic argument to the medieval legal codes that rule from them
--- constitutes a large, hand-curated, cross-register relevance dataset
assembled centuries before information retrieval existed. We extract
\textbf{50{,}214} such pairs from the Sefaria link graph, of which
\textbf{27{,}013} connect Maimonides' \emph{Mishneh Torah} to the Babylonian
Talmud at segment granularity with English on both sides, and use them to
fine-tune a retrieval model.

Measurement precedes training. We establish that lexical and dense retrieval
score almost identically on code-to-source retrieval (MRR $0.497$ vs $0.500$)
while succeeding on \emph{different} queries (top-10 overlap $63\%$, union R@10
$0.836$); that agent-derived semantic chunking into \emph{sugyot} produces a
large apparent gain which \emph{reverses sign} under a candidate-pool control;
and --- decisively --- that retrieval measured with code rulings as queries does
not transfer to the natural questions a user actually asks. That last finding
redirects the training data: anchors must be question-shaped, not ruling-shaped.

The resulting model, \textbf{torah-embed}, is fine-tuned from
\texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} on 33{,}393 triplets with same-daf hard negatives, and
evaluated on 552 queries from two entirely held-out tractates against the full
81{,}481-segment corpus. It improves MRR by $+12.2\%$ on ruling queries,
$+20.0\%$ on practical questions and $\mathbf{+35.9\%}$ on conceptual questions
--- a gain that rises monotonically as lexical overlap with the target falls
($0.41 \to 0.30 \to 0.21$). Against a pre-registered criterion requiring that
question-query gains exceed ruling-query gains, the model passes under both
credit rules ($+27.9\%$ vs $+12.2\%$ strict; $+28.9\%$ vs $+14.2\%$
sugya-level). Absolute performance remains modest, and we say so: conceptual
questions reach MRR $0.116$.

We report the measurements, the four designs they eliminated, and the five
errors we made obtaining them --- three of which inflated results and were
caught only by controls fixed in advance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval over the Talmud is an unusually well-posed problem with an unusually
awkward corpus. The Babylonian Talmud is roughly 1.8 million words of Aramaic and
Hebrew dialectic, organised not by topic but by association; a reader looking for
the source of a legal ruling must know where to look, and knowing where to look
is most of the skill.

The motivating application is a retrieval-augmented system that, given a
question, returns the passage of Talmudic argument bearing on it. Such a system
needs training data: pairs of (query, relevant passage) at sufficient volume to
fine-tune an embedding model. Prior work of ours \cite{mathembed} obtained such
pairs for mathematical retrieval by extracting a knowledge graph from the corpus
with a language model and deriving (concept, chunk) pairs from it. That
supervision was synthetic, approximate --- a concept mapped to \emph{every} chunk
of every relevant paper --- and its coarseness forced reliance on MRR and NDCG
because Recall@20 sat at 3--4\%.

The Talmud offers something better, and it is the observation this paper is built
around.

\subsection{\emph{Ein mishpat} is already a relevance dataset}

Printed in the margin of every page of the Vilna Talmud is a cross-reference
apparatus, \emph{ein mishpat / ner mitsvah}, compiled in the sixteenth century by
Joshua Boaz. It maps each passage of Talmudic argument to the places in the
medieval legal codes --- Maimonides' \emph{Mishneh Torah}, the \emph{Tur}, the
\emph{Shulchan Arukh} --- that rule from it. Sefaria has digitised this apparatus
as typed links at segment granularity.

This is, in modern terms, a relevance dataset with properties one would struggle
to buy:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Hand-curated} by domain experts, over centuries, with no
      model in the loop.
\item \textbf{Cross-register by construction.} One side is terse codified law;
      the other is discursive dialectic. They share little surface vocabulary,
      so the pairs test semantic rather than lexical matching.
\item \textbf{Segment-level, not document-level.} Relevant sets are small ---
      mean $2.6$ targets per source --- so Recall@$k$ is a sharp metric rather
      than the blunt instrument it became in \cite{mathembed}.
\item \textbf{Exactly the deployment task.} ``Where does this ruling come from''
      is the question the apparatus was built to answer.
\end{itemize}

The analogy to SPECTER2 \cite{specter2} is close and, we think, load-bearing:
SPECTER2 improves a scientific encoder by contrastive training on citation
triplets. \emph{Ein mishpat} is a citation graph. What follows is an attempt to
establish, by measurement, whether that analogy survives contact with the data.

\subsection{Contributions}

This is a measurement paper and its contributions are measurements.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item A census of the available supervision: 50{,}214 cross-register pairs,
      and a coverage analysis showing which parts of the canon are usable in
      English at all (\S\ref{sec:census}).
\item Baselines showing that lexical and dense retrieval are equal in aggregate
      and complementary in detail (\S\ref{sec:baselines}).
\item A negative result: agent-derived semantic chunking does not improve
      retrieval, and the apparent improvement inverts under a proper control
      (\S\ref{sec:chunking}).
\item A query-distribution result: the benchmark task is not the deployed task,
      and the gap is large but partly an artifact of metric granularity
      (\S\ref{sec:questions}).
\item A methodological account, including five errors we made and how each was
      caught (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Two pair types are unusable, for opposite reasons}

\emph{Shulchan Arukh} pairs are numerous but only 855 of 15{,}860 have English on
the source side. They are dropped.

Mishnah pairs are well covered but \textbf{lexically duplicate}: the Gemara
quotes each mishnah verbatim at the head of its sugya and the Davidson edition
reproduces the quotation, so source and target are frequently the same English
sentences. Mean token overlap is $0.62$, with $34/158$ sampled pairs above $0.9$.
Training on them teaches copy detection.

They are nonetheless retained, \textbf{as a positive control}: a correctly wired
lexical retriever must score near ceiling on duplicate pairs. It does
(\S\ref{sec:baselines}), which is how we know the harness is sound.

\emph{Mishneh Torah} pairs are clean by the same measure: mean token overlap
$0.42$, and none of 171 sampled pairs above $0.9$. This is the gold set:
\textbf{27{,}013} pairs over $\approx 9{,}500$ distinct queries with English on
both sides --- two orders of magnitude more supervision than \cite{mathembed}
was trained on, and human rather than model derived.

\section{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

\subsection{Setup}

The corpus is the full English Bavli: \textbf{81{,}481} segments, mean 99 words,
median 96, 90th percentile 149. Segments are short enough that no chunking is
required for a 512-token encoder.

Queries are source-side texts; relevance is the link. Retrieval is over the
entire corpus with no tractate hint or candidate pruning. We report BM25
($k_1=1.5$, $b=0.75$) and \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} \cite{bge} used zero-shot,
with the prescribed query instruction prefix.

Scoring 0.497 and 0.500, the two methods look interchangeable. They are not.

which is false. The useful question about any retriever here is not whether the
average moved but \emph{which queries it fixes}.

\section{Semantic chunking: a negative result}\label{sec:chunking}

\subsection{Motivation and protocol}

A Sefaria segment averages 99 words and an \emph{amud} holds 15.2 of them, while
a \emph{sugya} --- a complete unit of argument --- spans five to fifteen. Every
indexed unit is therefore a fragment of an argument. We tested whether merging
segments into sugyot improves retrieval.

Boundaries were chosen by language-model agents that read the text. Nine agents
processed 32 amudim of tractate Berakhot under a protocol designed for
verifiability:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item Agents return \textbf{reference ranges, never text}, making corruption of
      the corpus structurally impossible.
\item Boundaries fall only at existing segment edges; since Sefaria segments
      never break mid-sentence, mid-sentence cuts cannot occur.
\item Output must \textbf{partition} the input: every segment in exactly one
      chunk. This is checked mechanically.
\item Each chunk carries a generated heading giving its context in the
      surrounding argument, prepended to the body before embedding.
\item Headings may not name any later legal code, since the evaluation measures
      exactly the link to those codes.
\end{itemize}

The protocol produced 216 chunks with \textbf{zero partition failures} and zero
heading leaks. Merge ratio was 26.4 segments to 6.8 chunks per amud.

\subsection{The control matters more than the result}

Chunking reduces the candidate pool (844 segments $\to$ 209 chunks in the
evaluated region). A smaller pool mechanically improves rank-based metrics. We
therefore report both raw metrics and \textbf{matched-fraction recall}: the top
1\% of the pool in each condition (8 segments vs 2 chunks), which also
approximately matches retrieved text volume.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
Condition & Method & Pool & MRR & R@10 & \textbf{R@1\%} \\
\midrule
A. Segments & BM25 & 844 & 0.580 & 0.788 & \textbf{0.747} \\
A. Segments & dense & 844 & 0.527 & 0.818 & \textbf{0.818} \\
B. Sugya chunks & BM25 & 209 & 0.683 & 0.919 & 0.677 \\
B. Sugya chunks & dense & 209 & 0.587 & 0.838 & 0.596 \\
C. Chunks + heading & BM25 & 209 & 0.681 & 0.919 & 0.687 \\
C. Chunks + heading & dense & 209 & 0.595 & 0.838 & \textbf{0.646} \\
D. Heading only & dense & 209 & 0.415 & 0.707 & 0.384 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Chunking A/B, \emph{Mishneh Torah} queries, n=99, Berakhot 2a--17b.
Raw MRR and R@10 favour chunking; matched-fraction recall reverses the verdict.}
\label{tab:ab}
\end{table}

Read raw, chunking is a triumph: MRR $0.580 \to 0.683$, R@10 $0.788 \to 0.919$.
Under the matched-fraction control it is a \emph{loss}: dense R@1\% falls
$0.818 \to 0.646$.

The mechanism is dilution. A chunk containing the gold segment also contains
three or four others, and the embedding averages toward their mean. This is the
precision cost of context, and it is real.

We emphasise that the confound did not merely inflate the effect; \textbf{it
reversed its sign}. Reported without the control, this experiment would have
shown a ten-point gain and written semantic chunking into the system.

\subsection{Two results worth keeping}

Conditions B and C share a pool, so their comparison is unconfounded. Headings
help dense retrieval modestly and at no cost (R@1\% $0.596 \to 0.646$) and do
nothing for BM25, which cannot exploit context it can only match lexically.

Condition D is a \textbf{behavioural leakage detector}. If the agents had encoded
answers in the headings, heading-only retrieval would be excellent. It scores
MRR $0.415$ against $0.595$ for heading-plus-body --- substantially worse than
the text itself. This is stronger evidence than a string check, and we recommend
retaining such a condition whenever generated context is added to an index.

\subsection{Resolution: retrieve narrow, present wide}

The metric assumes segment-level precision, because \emph{ein mishpat} links
point at segments. Semantic chunking disputes that premise, arguing the natural
unit is the argument. The evaluation therefore encodes the assumption the design
contests.

We resolve this by separating the two roles. The \textbf{segment} remains the
indexing and retrieval unit; the \textbf{sugya} becomes the display and context
unit. The system retrieves a segment and returns the enclosing argument. This
takes the precision of fine granularity and the readability of coarse, and uses
the chunking work rather than discarding it.

\section{The benchmark task is not the deployed task}\label{sec:questions}

\subsection{A paired test}

Every measurement above uses a \emph{Mishneh Torah} ruling as the query. A
deployed system receives a question. To isolate phrasing from target, we took 40
rulings with known gold sugyot and had agents rewrite each as the question a
learner would ask, in plain English, avoiding the ruling's technical vocabulary,
then scored retrieval against \emph{the same} gold.

strict & BM25 (R@10) & 0.750 & 0.325 & $-57\%$ \\
strict & dense (R@10) & 0.725 & 0.500 & $-31\%$ \\
\midrule
sugya & BM25 (R@10) & 0.800 & 0.375 & $-53\%$ \\
sugya & \textbf{dense (R@10)} & 0.750 & \textbf{0.675} & $\mathbf{-10\%}$ \\
sugya & dense (MRR) & 0.607 & 0.346 & $-43\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Paired comparison, n=40, same gold targets. ``Sugya'' credit accepts a
retrieved segment within $\pm 3$ of a gold segment, approximating the unit the
system displays.}
\label{tab:questions}
\end{table}

\subsection{What survives}

Under strict scoring the collapse looks total. It is not, and the difference
matters for what to build.

\textbf{BM25 genuinely collapses}, on both metrics under both credit rules. Its
advantage in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} was an artifact of using rulings as
queries: a ruling shares vocabulary with its source in a way a question does not.

\textbf{Dense retrieval largely survives.} Scored at the granularity the system
displays, its recall falls only 10\%. What degrades is ranking: MRR falls 43\%.
Dense retrieval finds the right argument for a natural question nearly as often
as for a ruling, and cannot put it first.

Notably, token overlap with the gold passage is unchanged between query forms
($0.41$ vs $0.42$). The collapse is not explained by vocabulary loss but by
\emph{specificity}: a question is short and its words are common, so it carries
few discriminative terms.

\subsection{A third query distribution}

We also ran 26 questions drawn from the author's own writing --- theological and
historical rather than practical (\emph{Why did Jacob change his name to
Israel?}; \emph{Is the promise of the land conditional on how we behave?}). No
gold labels exist for these; results were inspected qualitatively and are
reproduced in full in \texttt{questions.pdf}.

Retrieval was substantially better than the benchmark predicted. Two examples:
\emph{Why did Jacob change his name to Israel?} returns Berakhot 13a:1, the sugya
on precisely that question; \emph{Who counts as the firstborn?} returns Bekhorot
47b:14 on firstborn status for inheritance.

Top-1 agreement between BM25 and dense was \textbf{2 of 26}, against 63\% on the
synthetic benchmark --- complementarity is far stronger on out-of-distribution
queries. On the Akedah question the two retrievers returned the two halves of a
two-part question: BM25 the sacrifice narrative, dense the promise of
descendants ``as the stars''. A fused ranking would have buried one of them.

\section{Method, and five errors}\label{sec:method}

We record our errors because each was caught by the same signal --- a number that
flattered the project --- and because the practices that caught them are the
transferable part of this work.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{A self-fulfilling denominator.} Coverage was computed as
      non-empty over segments-\emph{returned}; texts with no English version
      returned nothing and left the denominator. The metric could only ever
      report 100\%.
\item \textbf{An over-broad filter.} Counting every title under
      \texttt{Talmud > Bavli} as Talmud included commentaries \emph{on} the
      Talmud, inflating the census from 27{,}601 to 39{,}204 pairs. It was
      noticed because a commentary appeared in a ranking of tractates.
\item \textbf{A systematic constraint.} Requiring chunks to partition within an
      \emph{amud} forced every sugya crossing a page break to be severed --- the
      exact failure the exercise existed to prevent, occurring at all 5{,}349
      boundaries.
\item \textbf{An unrepresentative sample.} \emph{Shulchan Arukh} coverage
      sampled chapters 1, 50 and 150; the estimate was 89\% and the truth 5.4\%.
\item \textbf{A sign-reversing confound.} The chunking A/B without pool-size
      control (\S\ref{sec:chunking}).
\end{enumerate}

Four practices proved to carry their weight:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Pre-registration.} Acceptance criteria were written before results
      existed --- the chunking verifier before the agents returned, the
      decision rule before the dense baseline, the matched-fraction control
      before the A/B.
\item \textbf{Adversarial controls in every table.} The BM25 row as a
      lexical-leakage alarm; the Mishnah positive control; heading-only
      retrieval as a leakage detector.
\item \textbf{Structural rather than procedural guarantees.} Agents returning
      ranges rather than text cannot corrupt a corpus, whatever they do.
\item \textbf{Asking agents what they were unsure about.} Errors 3 above, and a
      prompt defect in which a stated ``typical'' chunk count became a target
      that fragmented arguments, were \emph{invisible} to every mechanical
      check: a size-motivated split and a severed sugya both partition
      perfectly. Both surfaced only through agents volunteering doubt about work
      that verified clean. An A/B on the corrected prompt confirmed the effect:
      23\% fewer chunks, never more on any amud.
\end{itemize}

\section{The model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Base:} \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} (110M parameters) --- the strongest
English retriever at BERT-base scale that fine-tunes within 16\,GB of unified
memory. \textbf{Loss:} MultipleNegativesRankingLoss. \textbf{Anchors:} both
ruling-shaped and question-shaped, the latter generated by language-model agents
in two registers (practical and conceptual) and oversampled $3\times$ to offset
their smaller number. \textbf{Negatives:} up to three per query drawn from the
\emph{same daf}, non-linked --- random in-batch negatives are trivially easy in
this corpus, since a random segment is another tractate on another subject.

Training: 33{,}393 triplets, 1 epoch, batch 16, max length 192, lr $2\times
10^{-5}$ with 10\% warmup, 2{,}087 steps in 1h25m on Apple MPS. Loss fell
$0.641 \to 0.303$. Batch size was forced by memory; the method prefers 64 or
more, and this is the most likely source of further gain.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol, fixed before training}

Splitting is by \textbf{whole tractate} --- Sanhedrin and Yevamot held out
entirely --- because splitting by link leaks: both endpoints of a held-out link
still appear via their other links. The 809 queries with targets on both sides
were \emph{discarded} rather than assigned. Retrieval is over all 81{,}481
segments with no pruning and no tractate hint; pruning to the held-out tractates
would shrink the pool $20\times$ and inflate every metric, which is precisely
the confound that reversed the chunking result in \S\ref{sec:chunking}.

\subsection{The pre-registered criterion}

Before training we fixed the following: \emph{the model must beat zero-shot on
held-out question queries by a margin exceeding its gain on ruling queries.} A
model improving only on rulings has learned the benchmark rather than the task
--- and since a ruling shares vocabulary with its source sugya in a way a
question does not, that is the easy win to optimise for accidentally.

Improvement rises monotonically as lexical overlap falls. The model gained most
exactly where surface matching helps least --- the signature of semantic
learning, and a pattern a memorising model cannot produce, since memorisation
would show the opposite gradient. Against BM25 the contrast is starker still:
$2.3\times$ on practical questions and $2.8\times$ on conceptual ones, where
BM25 reaches MRR $0.042$ and is effectively unusable.

\subsection{What this is not}

Absolute performance is modest. Conceptual questions reach MRR $0.116$ and R@1
$0.067$: the correct sugya ranks first roughly seven times in a hundred. This is
a measurable improvement on a hard task, not a solved problem.

Three further caveats. The held-out tractates are heavy in status-chain material
(levirate marriage, capital courts) that our question-writers independently
reported as hardest to phrase naturally; we verified the split is not biased on
living-law content ($0.28$ test vs $0.26$ train), but the task is genuinely
harder there. The test questions were generated from the rulings by the same
process as the training questions, so the \emph{relative} comparison is sound
while the absolute level is an optimistic bound. And one epoch at batch 16 is a
hardware compromise, not a considered choice.

\section{Conclusion}

The \emph{ein mishpat} apparatus is a hand-curated cross-register relevance
dataset of some fifty thousand pairs, assembled for exactly the retrieval task a
modern system wants to perform. Fine-tuning on it produces a model that is
better at the task users actually perform --- asking questions --- than at the
benchmark it was trained against, by a factor of more than two.

Equally load-bearing is what measurement eliminated: dense-only retrieval
(lexical and dense are complementary, union R@10 $0.836$ against $\approx 0.73$
each); sugya-level indexing (a ten-point apparent gain that inverts under a
pool-size control); ruling-anchored training (the benchmark task is not the
deployed task); and exact-segment evaluation (it scores a capability the product
does not provide, understating dense retrieval by roughly half). Each of the
four would have looked correct on an uncontrolled measurement, and one of them
looked correct on ours until the control was applied.
